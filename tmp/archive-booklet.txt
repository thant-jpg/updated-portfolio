As humans overused the Earth’s resources, depleting supplies of rare minerals and releasing harmful chemicals into 
the atmosphere, the ozone layer weakened, boosting solar radiation and making parts of the world uninhabitable. 
This forced migrations to safer areas, including underground, where people encountered both friendly and hostile life 
forms. Some adapted through cooperation, others through developing immunity. These changes sparked advances 
that extended lifespans and enabled further habitation across the planet. This human propagation also led to people 
looking to the world’s oceans in an attempt to harness new resources, a new frontier growing ever larger as the sea 
levels continued to rise. Eventually, climate change reached a peak and pushed humanity into a sparse enough state 
that the remaining populations agreed to completely decentralise and democratise the natural resources they had left. 
The usage of AI for adaptation to disease brought about advances in biotechnology. One thing led to another, and sud-
denly AI was able to perfectly model all kinds of physiological processes, even creating digital clones. AI also devel-
oped new methods to communicate with each other, starting to collate or compare information more efficiently than 
ever. But as the medical potential and overall capabilities of AI increased, so did its demand; and with its increased 
utility and emergence as a world-altering power, corporations monopolised access to it, turning it into a commodity. 
Misinformation ran wild in some places, with corrupt governments leveraging it as a tool to manipulate the people 
with the help of AI. This led to the formation of mental health retreats and led some communities to revert back to 
penning their lived experiences in a rejection of AI. However, as AI continued to progress at a rapid pace, its applica-
tions for censorship and revisionism marched forward, being able to completely alter video footage from the past and 
present to continually manipulate people. Stronger resistances formed in the wake of this oppression of information, 
and by using the AI tools they could get their hands on and creating some of their own, the people revolted through 
free journalism and unseen editing of government-sanctioned media.
As humans continued to rapidly develop cures to various ailments, life expectancies grew uncontrollably, and the pop-
ulation aged significantly, with 4-5 generations of people coexisting in some regions. Linguistics became slurred by 
a multitude of fusions with the fast-paced and layered abbreviations derived from ever-adapting brainrot content, 
changing the way people communicated their thoughts and feelings on a daily basis. Polynesian communities expe-
rienced an erosion of their traditions, moving into caves, and repurposing them as spaces of revival through creating  
a collection of sounds and actions. As a result, other cultures and even people interested in preserving culture sought 
to use music as a vehicle, creating repositories for all types of music. Rhythms became an integral as a way of record-
ing unfiltered and uncensored thoughts and emotions, adding a new dimension to the way we understand ourselves. 
Despite basic resources now being shared, the battles for rights over information continued, and inequality therefore 
appeared under a new guise, altering the way people interacted and formed their communities.

Nexus: A Brief History of Information Networks
The author elaborates on how information networks, much like traditional libraries, are double-edged spaces that both 
empower and constrain. Harari illustrates how digital networks promise unprecedented access to knowledge, connection, 
and creativity, fostering a sense of global community and democratized information. However, he also warns that these 
same networks are embedded with mechanisms of surveillance, algorithmic control, and corporate or governmental gate-
keeping.
The architecture of the network determines not only what information is accessible, but also how it is filtered, prioritized, 
and interpreted, often in ways that are invisible to the user. This dynamic reveals that information is never entirely neu-
tral; it is shaped by those who design and manage the networks, reflecting broader societal dynamics of power, influence, 
and inequality. 
The author points out that throughout history, empires, churches, and governments have curated, manipulated, and weap-
onized information to maintain order, build narratives, and suppress dissent. 
He warns about the rise of AI and digital platforms, which introduce a new layer of complexity: for the first time, non-hu-
man entities can autonomously decide what stories we encounter and share, potentially manipulating beliefs and behaviors 
on a massive scale.

These grasslands had sustained nomadic life for over a millennium, but by the early 2050s, the land was failing. Weather 
patterns had shifted out of balance; summers stretched longer, and winters were harsher. The decay came slowly, season 
by season. 
By 2060, even the most resilient nomadic communities could no longer deny reality. Most were rehoused in cities by 2063. 
The government’s emergency housing program was meant to be a temporary solution until the weather patterns stabilized. 
But they never did. 
The Last Nomads ( 2055-2063 ) 
After the earlier stages of the silicon crisis in the 2070s, many governments decided to abandon global climate restoration 
efforts and focused on fortifying their cities. As forests stood thinner and quieter, cities rose higher. 
Life continued in the city.  
These megastructures were optimized to conserve energy, recycle water, and manage resources efficiently. Daily life was 
tracked by Platform-n to calculate and ration resource use. Control was not optional. In a world of scarcity brought about 
by environmental collapse, a tightly controlled system was essential to ensure humanity’s long-term survival. 
Life wasn’t unbearable. Some adapted and even thrived.  
But others could not. 
The Vertical Cities ( 2063-2075 ) 
It was a gnawing discontent that grew day by day until it was unbearable. The routines repeated and daily life felt too rigid 
and controlled. Amidst the tedium and discontent, whispers of old tales drifted back; tales of lives that used to move with 
the wind and follow the stars. These stories began to surface again as possibilities.  
The Remembering ( 2073-2075 ) 
Life outside the city wasn’t easy. The land had changed, and they had to explore a new sustainable migration pattern. 
Technology had to be portable and used sparingly: solar cell fabric, kinetic chargers, compact water filters, portable water 
condensers.  
These nomads traveled in small groups using hybrid animals, repurposed vehicles, and even on foot. They lived light. What 
they couldn’t carry, they didn’t keep. Shelters were modular and temporary, made with solar textiles, salvaged metal and 
bamboo-like composites; collapsible, designed for constant movement. They traded when needed, bartered with isolated 
settlements, and repaired rather than replaced.

The future of nomadism will likely be shaped by continued technological innovation, evolving work cultures, and a growing 
emphasis on community and sustainability. As global challenges like climate change and urbanization intensify, the adap-
tive strategies of both traditional and modern nomads offer valuable insights for resilient, flexible living in an increasingly 
unpredictable world.
Future of Nomadism
Nomadism traces its roots to humanity’s earliest survival strategies. For ancient nomads, the primary goal was the pursuit 
of natural resources: mainly food and water. The scarcity and unpredictability of these resources in any single location led 
groups, rather than individuals, to travel together, ensuring greater safety and efficiency in resource gathering. This col-
lective movement fostered strong social bonds and interdependence within tribes.
Origins and Purpose of Nomadism
The choice to live nomadically is deeply tied to environmental and economic factors. Limited local resources, changing cli-
mates, and the need for fresh pastures or water sources drive migration. Nomads often follow established seasonal routes, 
forming mental maps of their territories that guide their annual movements. This cyclical migration allows them to sus-
tainably manage resources and adapt to environmental shifts.
Motivations for a Nomadic Lifestyle
Nomadic groups rarely wander aimlessly; their migrations are often predictable and regionally contained. Movement typ-
ically follows a circular or elliptical path, shaped by generations of experience and environmental knowledge. Over time, 
technological advancements, such as carts, barges, tents, yurts, and early caravans, have made mobility more efficient and 
comfortable, reflecting the ingenuity of nomadic cultures in adapting to their landscapes.

This medium is extremely durable as it is able to withstand temperatures up to 1,000°C, immersion in water, exposure to chemicals, and even

Durability
The exponential growth of digital data has exposed the limitations of traditional storage media, such as hard drives and 
magnetic tapes, which are vulnerable to environmental factors, electromagnetic fields, and bit rot.
Exponential Growth of Digital Data
Fused Quartz: The process starts with fused quartz, which is made by heating natural quartz (silicon dioxide) to extremely 
high temperatures (often above 2,000°C) until it melts and then cooling it to form a highly pure, glass-like solid. The sur-
face of the fused quartz is polished to ensure it is smooth.
Quartz As a Data Storage Medium
Data is written into the quartz using femtosecond lasers, which emit extremely short and intense pulses of light. The laser 
pulses create permanent nanostructures (called voxels) in multiple layers inside the quartz. Each voxel’s position (X, Y, Z), 
size, and orientation (birefringence) encode information, resulting in five-dimensional (5D) data storage.
Data Writing
Specialized polarization-sensitive microscopes are used to read the data. These devices analyze how polarized light inter-
acts with the nanostructures, decoding the stored information without causing any wear or damage.

In a world that is ever changing due to shifting climates and scarce resources, the deep library drifts across the land. Un-
bounded by walls, the nomadic sanctuary carries the weight of humanity’s most precious knowledge on rare, irreplaceable 
quartz shards. With limited storage, the library embodies a challenge. What knowledge is truly essential when not every-
thing can be preserved? In such a situation where everything cannot be saved, wisdom lies not in remembering all, but in 
choosing what must endure. 
The Wandering Archive 
As a central hub, there is a main library that remains stationary utilizing a network of satellites that orbit the Earth to col-
lect up-to-date climate data. Alongside it, smaller mobile libraries travel to different locations, gathering localized climate 
data and engaging directly with communities. Together, these stationary and moving libraries ensure a rich, diverse, and

Network
For wandering archives, climatic data is collected through sensors, observation, satellites, through the network of other 
mobile libraries and also from unformal discussions with the locals. Data collected is then condensed and selected through 
a systematic process that prioritizes human survivability in different climate conditions. These data are engraved onto 
quartz shards. 
Data
Engraved quartz shards are on display for the visitors to browse and read. If a visitor wishes to keep a dataset, they can 
request to have the information extracted onto another medium for personal use. The library also encourages community 
engagement. Visitors can contribute their own localized data or insights through informal exchanges in the Discussion Cir-
cle, enriching the archive and fostering collaborative knowledge.

To understand how sunlight enters and moves across the cave, I carried out a solar inci-
dent radiation analysis on the 3D model. This simulation measured how much solar ex-
posure different surfaces received over time, with warmer colors showing high radiation 
and cooler colors indicating shaded areas. By identifying which areas remain in shadow 
for most of the day, I could determine suitable zones for cooler, more controlled library 
functions such as archives, while sunlit areas were reserved for spaces like entrances or 
gathering platforms. This analysis helped align spatial placement with the cave’s natu-
ral light conditions, reducing the need for artificial intervention.

The platforms and massing were shaped directly in response to the cave’s contours, aligning their edges and volumes with 
the natural rock surfaces to minimize disruption and emphasize the organic geometry of the site. Each block was adjust-
ed to nest into flatter zones revealed during surface analysis, allowing the architecture to feel embedded rather than im-
posed. After defining the main program volumes, I introduced circulation for the librarians, shown in green, which threads 
through the spaces as elevated pathways and connectors. This circulation not only ensures efficient movement between 
work areas but also weaves the different levels into a coherent network within the irregular cave form.
Orange Spaces = public and collective functions of the archive, including the reading chambers, 
discussion circles, and the main archive storage. These spaces are designed as communal an-
chors where knowledge is accessed, shared, and preserved.
Blue Spaces = the data processing zones, such as engraving stations and transcription areas, 
where raw information is refined, condensed, and inscribed onto quartz shards.
Purple Spaces = the data collection systems, including sensors, observation decks, and quality 
control, which bring in external climatic data and ensure its reliability before entering the ar-
chive.
